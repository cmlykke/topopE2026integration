""""
Service A (the owner of the referenced row) should publish the events. Service B should subscribe.
After you split the tables, there is no real foreign-key constraint anymore — only a stored ID. Consistency becomes eventual and is done with domain events, not with a database constraint.
Why A notifies B

A owns the authoritative record. When that record is created, updated in a meaningful way, or deleted, A is the only place that knows it first.
B only holds a copy of the ID (and often a small denormalized snapshot). B needs to react: mark its rows orphaned, cancel related work, update a local cache, etc.
Classic examples: Customer service publishes CustomerDeleted / CustomerUpdated; Order service listens and handles the dangling customer_id. Product catalog publishes price/availability changes; anything that stored a product ID listens.

This is the usual direction in the database-per-service + event-driven pattern.
When B might notify A
Only if A actually cares about the existence or state of B’s rows (for example A keeps a counter, an “has active children” flag, or must refuse deletion while children exist). That is a separate, explicit business rule, not something the old FK automatically gave you. Most simple “B points at A” cases do not need it.
